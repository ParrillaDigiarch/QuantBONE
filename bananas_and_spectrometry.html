Seeing the invisible

Light, materials and Machine Learning: how to estimate what is inside a sample without destroying it.

°Brix = Unit of measurement indicating the quantity of sugar per 100 grams of solution (2°Brix = 2% sugar).

We can tell how sweet a banana is without opening it, just by its colours (interaction with light).

Spectroscopy allows us to analyse colours more closely, including some invisible interactions (infrared, ultraviolet).

Light reflected by materials contains information about its chemical composition, like in the bananas (see study).

We will do the same with bones.

01

Measuring without damaging the sample

The spectrometer lights up the sample with a halogen lamp, similar to the ones at home, and measures the light that bounces back. No cutting, no grinding, no preparation, and each measurement takes a few seconds.

It is what our eyes do when we see a colour, but much more precisely.

This is how colour works: a red object absorbs all colours except red, which it reflects back to our eyes.

02

Light has colours we cannot see

Light can be split into wavelengths, measured in nanometres (nm). Our eyes only see from about 400 nm (violet) to 750 nm (red). Our spectrometer measures from 350 nm (ultraviolet) to 2500 nm (infrared).

What humans see is only a small band of what the instrument measures. Other instruments measures different ranges (for example, FTIR goes from 2500 to 25000 nm).

03

If it is so fast and harmless, why is it not used more?

Other techniques, such as FTIR or Raman, see molecules directly: each molecule leaves its signature in a specific place. VisNIR, the range we work with, does not see the molecule itself, but its echoes (overtones), weaker signals that appear at other wavelengths and blend together.

FTIR / Raman

Each molecule in its own place

VisNIR

Broad echoes that overlap

On the other hand, VisNIR is portable, fast and cheaper. But reading those echoes by eye is almost impossible. We need help.

04

This is where Machine Learning comes in

We measure many samples in both ways, with light (fast and harmless) and in the lab (accurate, but destructive). The computer looks for the patterns in the spectrum that go with each result. We call this a Machine Learning model.

Before we trust it, the model takes a test: we show it spectra of samples it has never seen and compare its answers with the real lab results. If it passes, we can use it on new samples and get an estimate in seconds.

05

Nothing new: it already works in other fields

Cereals

Protein and moisture in grain. The classic use of near-infrared, for decades.

Fruit

Sugar and ripeness without cutting, right on the packing line.

Recycling

Sorting different types of plastic automatically on the conveyor belt.

Pharmacy

Checking that each pill contains what it should.

Agriculture

Soil organic matter and crop health, even from satellites.

06

So what is the catch?

A model only knows what it has learned: one specific material and one specific property. If we change either of them, we have to check again that it works.

This opens up a huge field: testing which properties of archaeological materials can be estimated without destroying them.

Burnt bone → what temperature? Flint → was it heated? Pottery → firing? Sediment → organic matter?
We do not replace the lab. We learn from it, so we can look at more samples, select them for further analysis, and speed up the process without damaging them.
